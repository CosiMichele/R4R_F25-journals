### Rose

This week’s reading on data management really helped me see that what seems like “boring housekeeping” actually underpins everything you do with data, even in coursework. Even though I’m not doing independent research yet, in my lab classes I constantly generate tables, measurements, experiment logs, etc. The reading reminded me that good data management isn’t just a future concern, I can apply it now to make my life easier.

### Bud

One practical idea is to experiment with a mini data management plan (DMP) even for a class assignment. For example, before starting a lab, I could write a short plan: how I’ll collect the data, where I’ll store it, how I’ll back it up, how I’ll label files, and how I’ll document units and context. Even a 200-word plan would start me thinking ahead and probably avoid confusion later.
Also, I want to start a metadata habits log, for each dataset I produce in the class, I’ll include a small description file (maybe a .md or .txt) that states what each column means, units, how I collected it, and any anomalies. Over time, this will feel normal and could carry over into more formal research later.

## Research Application

Even though I’m not working on independent research yet, the data management concepts are still deeply applicable to my lab/coursework:

Planning: Before I run experiments, I can plan out file names, folder structure, and data documentation. That way I’m not scrambling later to reconstruct what I did.

Describing: I’ll include context (units, instrument settings, date/time, methods) alongside the raw data. That means if I or someone else revisits it later, they won’t be lost.

Backups: As I collect data across weeks, I risk losing files. I’ll commit to backing up lab data (for example, copying to my personal cloud or external drive). If I ever switch computers, I won’t lose months of lab work.

Sharing with credit: Sometimes I share data or graphs with lab partners or classmates. If I attach a simple license (or at least state how others may use it), I reduce confusion or misuse.

Scalability: As assignments get more complex, these practices will scale. If I ever move to real research, the habits will already be in place.
