### Rose
In Long COVID research, the principles of Open Science are highly relevant and strengthen both the rigor and impact of my work. Several pillars of Open Science have been especially applicable:
•	Open Access Publications have ensured that findings on Long COVID are freely available to researchers, practitioners, and policymakers worldwide. This fosters timely translation of evidence into practice and facilitates the identification of research gaps.
•	Open Educational Resources have provided valuable opportunities to learn new tools and stay updated on recent methodological advances and software, significantly aiding my research training and workflow.
•	Open-source statistical tools and software, such as R, make my analyses more reproducible, accessible, and cost-effective, while also enabling collaboration across research teams.

### Thorn
Two principles of Open Science that I believe may be more challenging to implement in my reserach are Open Peer Review and Open Data sharing. While the goal of transparency in peer review is commendable, it raises concerns about potential biases and whether junior researchers like me can provide critiques openly without unintended professional repercussions. Similarly, Open Data sharing is powerful for fostering collaboration and validation but presents complex challenges in sensitive health research like mine. Protecting participant confidentiality, navigating IRB restrictions, and managing multi-institutional data ownership are all significant hurdles that require careful consideration before broad implementation.

### Bud
Looking forward, one area with strong potential for application is the expanded use of Open-Source Software. While already adopted in many data science–driven public health labs, I believe greater encouragement is needed for researchers in my field to consistently integrate tools such as R and Python. Doing so can make analytic processes more transparent, collaborative-ready, and sustainable. In parallel, although Open Data presents challenges, I see its potential value: when de-identified data are responsibly shared, it not only validates findings but also fosters innovation and deeper investigation into chronic conditions and social determinants of health.

In the last Roots for Resilience Thursday Discussion, we learned about Github and chatted with Nirav Merchant about our different research and briefly about the application of AI tools in our research. For Long COVID studies, Github can serve as a powerful platform for collaboration and transparency by enabling version control of analytic code, data processing scripts, and even manuscript drafts. I think that sharing code on Github will not only enhance reproducibility but will also allow other researchers to adapt, improve, or build upon existing methods, which is particularly valuable in a rapidly evolving area like Long COVID. It also facilitates teamwork by tracking contributions, managing workflows, and integrating with AI tools that can help streamline data cleaning, analysis, and visualization. In combination, Github and AI support a more **open**, efficient, and scalable research process that can accelerate progress in understanding the long-term impacts of COVID-19 among individuals within a community.
